  9. Lee Ravitz · 01:53 14 Oct 2009

    Fascinating - I have been enjoying this whole series of new articles, but I think this is the most fully rounded so far. I wondered at the time of 'It Felt Like a Kiss' whether certain topics flagged up in that work would be pursued further, and I am glad to see that many of the central themes of that work *are* being expanded upon.

    The whole period of US led and/or inpsired techno-utopianism in the mid-late 50's is a most remarkable aspect of recent world history that I think is generally underplayed in most accounts of the era (though I believe that you were discussing some of the ramifications as early as 'Pandora's Box'). It seems to have had its effect massively across the world at this time - in affecting social and cultural developments in Argentina and Brazil, in Israel, in India, in Japan - as well as in Africa and the wider Middle East, and much less tends to get examined about it than about the contemporary drives towards USSR inspired Communist revolutions (perhaps a legacy of self-serving Cold War propaganda?). Even seemingly innocuous elements like the spread of the 'International Style' in architecture or the growth in the glamour of international air travel appear to have a peculiar place within this complex story.

    It is interesting that, in a sense, there seems to have been as much an 'engineering race' between the US and USSR as there was an 'arms race' and a 'space race'. After all, the cult of the heavy electrical engineer remained paramount in the Soviet Union until its dissolution.

    As a historian myself, I was particularly interested in Toynbee's contribution - certainly, he was a most preceptive man, although it should be remembered, pace Spengler, that one of his most fondly cherished ideas was the notion that civilizations effectively rise and fall in cyclical patterns that are, in some sense, predetermined. Also, and even more importantly, that particular 'culture areas' of the world are dominated by their own peculiar 'geist' or 'cultural soul' (for want of a better term). Therefore, no doubt his shock, in part, was due to encountering such a blantantly Western superimposition upon an essentially Middle Eastern polity - this, in itself, may be seen as a form of superstition. Still, whatever the basis of the interpretation it seems to have led to real insights.

    As to Rostow, another interesting figure - and one who firmly belongs within a time scale that produced similar luminaries in the US, as Galbraith, C. Wright Mills, Vance Packard et. al - who each seem to throw significant light upon the theorisations of their contemporary fellows.

    Keep them coming!

    Lee.
